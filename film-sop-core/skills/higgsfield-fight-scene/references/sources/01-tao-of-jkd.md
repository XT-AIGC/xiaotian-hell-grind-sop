# 01 — Tao of Jeet Kune Do (expanded, Russian edition 2014)

**Type:** primary-source book, Bruce Lee (notes from 1970–73, compiled by Linda Lee and Gilbert Johnson; expanded edition with commentary by Shannon Lee, Ted Wong, and others)

**What it adds to choreography:**
The complete mechanics of JKD in the first person: stance, footwork, all hand and leg strikes with trajectories and targets, the five ways of attack, timing, and broken rhythm. Establishes the style's visual code: minimalism, no windup or telegraphing, striking from any position, constant small movements, an explosion half a beat ahead of the opponent. The key to staging: a JKD fighter almost never attacks head-on—he intercepts, feints, and counters; the face is calm, the movement a “cobra's strike.” Lee's terminology (stop-hit, broken rhythm, SAA/HIA/PIA/ABC/ABD) provides a ready-made language for shot lists.

---

## Stance (on-guard position; in the JKD tradition—bai jong)

- “A small, dynamic stance on bent knees”: a half squat, narrow comfortable steps, a continuous cycle of micromovements—not a static pose. The center of gravity is slightly forward and down.
- Stronger side FORWARD (a right-handed person uses a right lead). The lead hand and leg deliver ~80% of strikes—they are already “halfway to the target.”
- Head: chin lowered 2–3 cm, lead shoulder raised 2–3 cm—they “meet halfway.” Only the crown is exposed; the tip of the chin is protected.
- Lead hand: loose, slightly low, CONSTANTLY moving to confuse the opponent—“flickering back and forth like the tongue of a snake ready to strike,” “like lightning.” Extension and return resemble a foil thrust.
- Rear/guard hand: elbow lowered in front of the lower ribs, forearm covering the solar plexus, open palm facing the opponent on the line of the lead shoulder; it can make circular, wave-like motions.
- The torso aligns with the lead foot: foot turned inward—a narrow target (defense); outward—a frontal position (for certain attacks).
- Rear heel raised—“the piston of the entire fighting machine”; weight on the balls of the feet. Lead heel barely touches the floor.
- Feet a natural step apart, always underneath the body, NEVER crossing.
- Compactness: “elbows, forearms, fists, and forehead form a single bundle… you resemble a cat arching its back, ready to spring”; “like a cobra—a loose but compact position.”
- The stance gives nothing away: “the pose reveals no more about the movements than a good poker player's face reveals about his cards.”
- Identifying frame: “A good fighter always moves as if his trousers were falling down” (knees continually half bent).

## Footwork

- “The essence of fighting lies in the art of movement.” Mobility above all: JKD has no “three years standing in kiba-dachi”; firmness is sought in living movement.
- Four basic movements: advancing, retreating, circling right, circling left.
- Forward/backward shuffle: a series of short steps, feet literally sliding along the floor without lifting, body continually in the on-guard position.
- Quick advance: a wide step + immediate recovery of the other foot; the body travels PARALLEL to the floor without bouncing—this is not a jump.
- Quick retreat; advance-retreat step—a body feint to open the opponent up.
- Side step: right/left ~40 cm; evade the strike, not the lunge; executed at the very last moment, when the strike has almost arrived. The foot nearest the direction of travel always moves first.
- “Forward fall”: moving sideways while shifting the torso forward, head tucked, hands high—entry into close combat.
- Rules: short, quick steps (two medium steps are better than one long one); controlled center of gravity; in hand attacks, the hand starts BEFORE the foot; the lead foot does not land until the fist touches the target.
- Movement quality: “move like an elegant ballroom dancer—the impression is that he glides across the floor”; “feet are springs”; the torso is “a turret carrying artillery”: stable however its base moves.
- Evasions (without leaving the “line of fire”): inside/outside slip (the opponent's punch passes over the shoulder; the key is turning the corresponding heel and shifting weight); duck (bending from the waist beneath a swing or hook); snap back (a sudden torso movement to make the punch fall short); roll (absorbing a strike by moving with it: backward against a straight, sideways against a hook, backward against an uppercut, circularly downward against a hammer); weaving (head as a moving target; relaxation is the secret; do not sway rhythmically like a pendulum).
- Slipping outside a straight is safest and leaves both hands free to counter. “Move in rather than lean away: let the opponent's hook pass behind your neck.”

## Hands

- Main principle: “you strike with your whole body”—power travels from the floor through the legs, waist, back, shoulder, and wrist. Power comes from rotating at the waist around the straightened lead leg (“the axis”), not from swaying.
- “A punch is not a push. A real punch is like the crack of a whip”; accelerate through the finish, clench the fist only on contact, focus “several inches BEHIND the target”—strike through the opponent.
- Lead straight punch—the “backbone of JKD hand technique.” Travels from the middle of the chest along the nose line, vertical fist with a slight “screwing” of the wrist. NO windup, no drawing back to the hip/shoulder, delivered from wherever the hand happens to be; returns along the same path, hand remaining high. The fastest and most accurate punch.
- Lead jab: “reconnaissance in force,” “a whip, not a club”; Ali: “swatting flies with a flyswatter.” Sharp, stinging, immediately withdrawn.
- Finger jab: “JKD's longest and fastest hand weapon,” targeting the eyes. “Fencing without a sword”; “looks like a cobra's strike—the opponent should feel it before he sees it.”
- Rear cross: “heavy artillery,” strictly straight, large waist rotation, shoulder and heel turn together—the “door-slamming principle”; weight transfers onto the lead leg at contact.
- Rear-hand straight to the body: a counterpunch with a squat to target level; the head moves onto the striking arm's line.
- Lead hook: the best counterpunch; never wide—“loose, light, sharp”; no windup, elbow rises at the last moment; lead heel turned outward, body rotates on the balls of the feet; wrist firm, fist and forearm a “club with a thickened end.” A close-range weapon that curves around the guard hand, “often outside the opponent's field of vision.”
- Shovel hook: the shortest and most explosive; elbow tucked against the pelvic bone, fist at 45°, hip rises with a “shoveling” thrust—the punch penetrates the defense.
- Corkscrew hook: like a straight, but with a sharp wrist turn before contact, forearm parallel to the floor; travels behind the opponent's guard hand to the jaw.
- Uppercut: very short range only; bent knees straighten sharply into the punch; against a lowered head and “long-range punchers.” Useless against a fast, upright fighter.
- Other weapons: palm hook, backfist (high/middle/low/vertical), swings on a shortened arc, hammer strikes, reverse circular strikes (with fist base, forearm, elbow), elbows (upward, downward, backward, “crushing” from right/left), headbutt.
- Combinations: “one-two” (right jab—left cross), jab—uppercut, jab—hook; triples: two body punches, third to the head; “safety triple”—body-head-body (last strike to the same target as the first). Always finish a series with the lead hand; alternate high-low, low-high.
- Double punches (double jab) break the opponent's rhythm and open a combination.

## Legs

- Role: the longest weapon, first at long range; “a kick should resemble a cobra's strike—the opponent should feel it but not see it.”
- Requirements for every kick: powerful and easy to execute, accurate, sudden, economical, smooth without jerks, coordinated with other techniques, reflexive.
- Side kick: JKD's main kick. Variants—downward to shin/knee/thigh (low, safest, “in combat it is better directed downward”), horizontal to ribs/abdomen/kidneys, high, sliding, jumping, pivoting on the supporting leg. “The more heavily the opponent weights a leg, the more dangerous a kick to its knee becomes.”
- Shin/knee kick: straight, downward, inside-out, outside-in; “a strong demoralizing effect, making the opponent attack less confidently”; restores distance; often an irritating feint before a finger jab or straight punch.
- Hook kick (outside-in): high/middle/low; contact with instep, ball of foot, shin; the principal fast kick to groin and head. Reverse hook (inside-out).
- Front kick: to knee or groin; instep kick to groin—force directed upward; vertical hook—upward through the middle level.
- From savate: knee and torso fixed, heel contact—“extremely fast, powerful, and the most difficult to block.”
- Knee-driven snap (groin hook, reverse hook, upward snap, front kick)—put power into it; hip-driven thrust (side, back, frontal)—put speed into it.
- Rotational: spinning back kick (high/middle/low, jumping), 360° wheel kick; arcing heel kicks (leg tensed and bent); downward stamping kick (to shin/foot, at close range “to maim”).
- Knees: upward and inward, with lead and rear legs—close combat.
- Crouching sweeps—at the start of combat, in combination, as a counterattack; strikes against a grounded opponent—“accurate, crushing.”
- A master kicks “from a high position, a low position, or the floor” with equal ease; many kicks (hook, side, vertical) require no change to the defensive hand position—hands remain in guard.
- Grappling layer (for harsh scenes): throws, reaps, joint locks (wrist, elbow, leg, back), chokes (rear, side), takedowns, trapping holds; “prohibited techniques”—hair (control), foot stamp (maiming), bites/pinches (escape), groin grab.
- Strike targets: eyes, groin, shin/knee—JKD's three “cheap” targets; vulnerable points: temples, throat, solar plexus, ribs, groin, kidneys, coccyx, knees.

## Interception and timing (the heart of JKD—the “intercepting fist”)

- Stop-hit: delivered SIMULTANEOUSLY with the opponent's attack; anticipates, closes the attack line, follows a trajectory that interrupts his strike. Opportunities: opponent preparing to advance, his arm not yet extended, during his feint, between two movements in a combination, on his attacking step. Often with the torso moving forward—to meet him.
- Stop-kick to shin/knee against the advancing part of the body—a signature interception against a sweeping attack.
- Anticipatory strike / second-intention attack: provoke the opponent's stop-hit (through invitation, an obvious feint, a false attack)—then parry it with a simultaneous response.
- Attack on preparation: strike while the opponent is only preparing his own attack—his hands and thoughts are occupied with offense.
- Simultaneous defense and attack: “always try to hit while slipping—stepping inside the punch, you hit harder than when you block and hit”; parry + strike in one beat.
- Opponent's reaction time increases immediately after his technique, on inhalation, with distracted attention, or on loss of balance—attack windows.
- Broken rhythm: evenly matched fighters move in a shared rhythm; whoever breaks it with a slight sway or unexpected movement lands—the opponent automatically continues the old rhythm. “A strike outside the rhythm catches the opponent open.”
- Half-beat strike: attack while the opponent's movement is still unfinished. A kicking PIA uses the rhythm “ONE—and…”: the first attack is deep and concealed; the second half is an explosive kick.
- Slowing down as a weapon: a strike halted halfway and resumed after the opponent has already moved to parry is an extremely effective change of cadence.
- Tempo: “one beat in the cadence”—a moment of the opponent's psychological and physical helplessness; attack during dips in his concentration.

## Five ways of attack (Lee's final classification)

1. **SAA—simple angular attack** (SAA/SDA): a single strike from an unexpected angle, sometimes after a feint; distance prepared through footwork. “Hitting an opponent with a simple movement is a sign of the highest mastery.”
2. **HIA—hand immobilization attack** (HIA, trapping): holding the head (hair), arm, or leg at the moment of contact; the hold restrains the limb and opens a safe striking zone. Forearm control is a “destructive weapon.”
3. **PIA—progressive indirect attack** (PIA): a feint or unfinished strike draws the defense the wrong way; the attack itself travels forward in ONE movement without retreating, into the opened line. The feint consumes half the distance; the attack starts while the opponent's hand is moving in the opposite direction.
4. **ABC—attack by combination** (ABC): strikes flowing naturally from one to the next along several lines; the sequence “sets up” the opponent for the final knockout blow (triples, “safety triple”).
5. **ABD—attack by drawing** (ABD, drawing): offer an opening / readable action, draw the opponent into attacking—then counter using his momentum and inability to cover up.

## Ranges and cadence

- Fighting distance: opponents cannot reach each other without an energetic lunge; each has their own range. Constant movement = playing with the other's sense of distance while preserving one's own.
- Attack “where the opponent will be, not where he is.”
- The shin is the nearest, continually vulnerable target: against a kicker, range is measured “shin to shin.”
- Long range: kicks, finger jab, lead jabs, rear crosses. Close combat: hooks, shovel hooks, uppercuts, elbows, knees, trapping, throws; entry—“glide dancing around the opponent, weaving patterns of slips, ducks, and sways.”
- Gaze: at long range—into the opponent's eyes; at close range—at his legs or waist. Never blink or close the eyes.
- Cadence: speed matched to the opponent's; imposing your cadence means “leading” him. Excessive speed is harmful: the attacker “parries himself,” catching up with the defense.
- Feints: “long/short” rhythm; a two-feint attack—“long/short/long”; the perfected version—“short/long/short.” First feint deep and penetrating (to draw a parry), final strike short and decisive. “Long” does not mean “slow.” More than two feints is dangerous.
- Feint triggers: a hand wave, foot stamp, sudden shout—reflexively scatter attention. “JKD style is based on feints and the actions associated with them.”
- Parry vs block: a parry is a sudden LIGHT inward/outward motion that diverts a strike; “success depends on timing, not force”; executed at the last moment, hand moving no farther than necessary. A block is a last resort: “weakens the body, wastes energy, disrupts balance.” Parry types: simple, semicircular (high-low), circular (catching the wrist and taking it off line). A retreating parry starts simultaneously with the step.
- Manipulating the opponent's arm (attack preparation): beat—a sharp strike against the arm; bind—diagonal displacement from high line to low; lowering—downward displacement on the same side; envelopment—a circular displacement returning to the starting point; pressure. All are “luring into a trap before an indirect attack.”

## Qualities and aesthetics (quotations)

- Five kinds of speed: perceptual, mental, initiation (starting without telegraphing), execution, alteration. “Instinctive movement—the simplest—is the fastest and most accurate.”
- Power = strength × speed; a light fighter with fast movement hits like a heavyweight. Multistage acceleration: toe push → knees → torso → shoulder → forearm → wrist → fist; “the final phase of the strike should be the fastest.”
- Economy: no windup, no unnecessary movement; “movements economical and sudden, like a falling coin.”
- Good form = maximum effect with minimum movement and energy; “an experienced athlete's most important quality is ease of movement even at maximum effort.”
- Facial expression: “Behavior in combat should differ in no way from everyday behavior… no changes in facial expression. Nothing should reveal that a person is fighting to the death.” The gaze does not fix on one point; the eyes do not burn with rage.
- Key wording for the style's aesthetic:
  - “In movement, imitate water. In stillness, be like a mirror. Respond like an echo.”
  - “The opponent should feel your strike before seeing it.”
  - “Punch when you need to punch; kick when you need to kick.”
  - “Simplicity is the shortest distance between two points.”
  - “Using no way as way; having no limitation as limitation.”
  - “Find stillness in movement, like the moon in the waves.”
  - “Have the eye of an eagle, the cunning of a fox, the agility and caution of a cat, the aggression and ferocity of a panther, the lightning striking power of a cobra, and the endurance of a mongoose.”
  - “If only we could strike with our eyes! How much is lost on the long journey from the eye through the arm to the fist.”
  - “The movement of a Jeet Kune Do practitioner is like a flash of lightning.”
  - “Let the opponent tear your skin—and you will tear his flesh; let him destroy your flesh—you will break his bones.”
  - “The best defense is attack”; “a fight must always be fought with the head, not the hands and feet.”
  - “Empty your cup so that it may be filled.”
  - Ted Wong (afterword): “Whoever controls distance controls the fight”; “The essence of fighting is the art of movement.”
- Tactics as character: “box a wrestler, wrestle a boxer”; against a calm opponent—long feints, against an agitated one—short; a master “uses every opportunity and attacks every opening,” dominates and imposes his own rhythm.
