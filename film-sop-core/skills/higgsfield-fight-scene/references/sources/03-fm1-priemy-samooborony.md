# 03 — Bruce Lee's Fighting Method, vol. 1: Self-Defense Techniques
**Type:** primary-source book (scan, Russian edition 1994, “Zdorovye Naroda” / MABI “Kou Dao”; original—Bruce Lee's Fighting Method, © Linda Lee 1976, Ohara Publications)
**What it adds to choreography:** the foundation of “how Lee stands and moves”—precise JKD stance, the full movement vocabulary (shuffle, dash, burst, sidestep), power mechanics (striking “through” the target from the hip and rear leg), and speed mechanics (nontelegraphic strikes, broken rhythm). This underpins every scenario sequence in books 2–4: any character “in Lee's style” must move by these rules on screen or the style becomes unreadable.

## 0. Source condition
- pp. 1–50 (text): generally readable, blurry in places—notes preserve the meaning.
- pp. 52–101 (photo sequences): severely degraded scan; captions and photo numbers nearly illegible. Poses and situations are visible; described below as visual reference without exact captions.
- Volume structure: Introduction → Ch. I Basic Exercises → Ch. II Fighting Stance → Ch. III Footwork → Ch. IV Power Development → Ch. V Speed Development → photo supplement to all chapters.

## 1. General principles (from the introduction—directly into character briefs)
- The book was photographed in 1966; Lee did not want a cult of techniques: technique is a tool, not dogma. Train “as though an opponent were really in front of you”—the only way to become a master: commit to every strike.
- A good fighter = speed + power + sense of distance + ability to read intentions; persistent training decides everything, not “mysticism.”
- The best defense is distance and mobility rather than hard blocks: evading with the feet is easier than deflecting with the hands.
- Relaxation is the foundation: tense shoulders and clenched fists “eat up” speed; tension explodes only at contact.

## 2. JKD fighting stance (on-guard)—posing the figure in frame
- Half-bent, springy position: strong hand and leg (Lee's right) FORWARD—“the strong side does most of the work.”
- Chin tucked/lowered toward the collarbone, lead shoulder slightly raised—jaw sheltered; eyes always on the opponent.
- Front hand on the line between self and target, slightly below the shoulder; rear hand near the torso, covering ribs/side, palm open near the chin. Hands in micromotion (up/down like a snake)—nothing for the opponent's eyes to “lock onto.”
- Feet a natural step apart, rear heel slightly raised (spring); weight approximately on both legs, body aligned with the lead leg—a narrow silhouette, minimal openings.
- Knees soft, stance neither wide nor narrow: too wide = stable but slow, groin exposed; too narrow = fast but collapses under a strike.
- The chapter analyzes ~15 INCORRECT stances (photos 26–40)—a ready catalog of poses for extras/opponents: hands against sides (entire upper body open), both hands high (abdomen open), “extended” lunging stance (slow start), chest square to opponent (all targets open), crossed legs (no base), torso pitched forward over lead leg (cannot punch with rear hand), etc. Lee's opponents should stand exactly this way on screen—“incorrectly.”

## 3. Balance
- Balance is the stance's central element: all body parts correctly positioned relative to each other, weight between the legs, lead knee soft.
- Center of gravity flows between legs only during a strike or step; onto the front leg in attack, backward during evasion/defense.
- “Lightness in the feet”: feet seem to hover just above the floor, moving in small sliding steps, no jumps or crossing; immediate return to stance after every action.
- A good fighter can start in any direction from any phase of motion; lost balance = the moment he is hit (the dramatic logic of an absorbed strike on screen).

## 4. Footwork—movement vocabulary for animation
- **Forward shuffle / “movement”:** front foot slides ~10 cm forward, rear follows the same distance; torso and arms retain their positions. Backward is mirrored (rear foot moves first). Feet rise no more than a couple of centimeters above the floor.
- **Quick advance:** a series of short sliding steps preserving stance; up to three “portions” of distance per second; no vertical oscillation, both legs equally involved.
- **Quick retreat:** the same mechanism backward; Lee's long first step carried him back 2+ meters—the opponent “falls” into empty space.
- **Forward burst/lunge:** fastest way to cover distance: a surging lead-leg step (~3/4 second for 2.5 m), center of gravity flying over the front leg, rear catching up; combined with an upward slap/feint of the lead hand (as though pulling a rope in front of the opponent's face) to break concentration, and a simultaneous side kick. Land on one leg, instantly recover stance.
- **Backward burst:** mirrored retreat over the distance of two ordinary steps in one explosion; used against a swing/clinch.
- **Sidestep:** right—away from the “closed” side; left—50–80 cm off a straight punch's attack line; feet step without crossing, torso turns toward the opponent into a defensive position. Moving sideways is not escape but a way to hit while the opponent's attack misses.
- Principle: against a left-hander, move right (away from his right); against a right-hander, left; the art is shifting to the side from which your strike can reach and his no longer can.

## 5. Power mechanics—“selling” the force of a strike on screen
- Power does not come from the arm: a punch results from the entire body's work; the arm merely “holds” the force produced by hips and rear leg. The striking line is an imaginary straight line from one's center to the target; the fist starts from the body's center, not the shoulder.
- Vertical fist, contact with the lower three knuckles; wrist, elbow, shoulder aligned at contact; strike “through” the target, focus beyond the back of the opponent's head/body rather than on its surface.
- Lateral hip rotation through 90–180° is the main amplifier; practice on a sheet of paper (20–30 cm, suspended at chest height) and a heavy bag.
- Famous “2.5 cm punch” (one-inch punch): no windup, from a relaxed hand, explosion over the last 10 cm; fist rises after contact—the opponent flies back rather than “getting a flick.” Works only at close range.
- “Flowing energy” / chi sao (sticky hands, Wing Chun): elbow “stationary,” energy flowing from torso into lower forearm; arms relaxed yet cannot be pushed through—partners stand close, rotating forearms, each seeking a central opening. Excellent close-combat reference for “intertwined hands.”
- Side kick—Lee's strongest strike: he tossed a 5 cm board and broke it in midair; power = speed + body weight, heel strikes “through” the target, eyes on target, no arm flailing. Broad-impact force: the kick “throws the opponent's entire body back,” rather than cutting into one point.
- Weak strikes (for screen opponents): arm-only punch without body involvement; shoulder windup; striking off balance; striking with an excessively twisted torso and no recovery.

## 6. Speed and invisible attack
- **The strike that does not “telephone”:** no preparatory micromovements—no drawing the fist back, unloading weight, or flash in the eyes; hand starts before the torso. A twitch of even one centimeter warns the opponent. For cinema: Lee's attacks should emerge “from nowhere,” from an entirely neutral pose.
- **Backfist (lead-hand strike with the back of the fist)**—fastest strike: delivered at close range, relaxed hand “fires” from a few centimeters away; target—head. Classic sequence: chi-sao contact → descending backfist.
- **Lead straight to the head**—the main tool; combined from any position between waist and shoulder.
- **Leg speed:** Lee's side and round kicks were almost as fast as a jab; “it is interesting to watch him chase an opponent with raised legs while the other man is completely off balance.”
- **Broken rhythm:** vary a series' speed and rhythm, pause and explode; a strike half a beat earlier or later than expected lands cleanly.
- **Reaction/sense of attack:** exercises—watch a random person and “predict” their movements; the blade-of-grass-and-dog trick (pull away before it grabs); partner with a coin on the palm. In scenes this looks like “Lee begins his counter before the opponent's attack has taken shape.”
- Five fast movements in succession after a large step (up to 10 fast movements in a series)—a model for the scene's final “exchange.”

## 7. Kicking arsenal (speed/power chapters + photos)
- **Side kick:** both a stop-hit to the knee/shin of an extended leg and a battering-ram kick to the body. Staging: lift lead knee + pivot supporting foot, torso leans away for balance, strike with foot edge/heel. Low variant to the knee: “all the opponent's body weight is on his front leg; the strike cannot be weak and restricts movement.”
- **Hook/round kick:** delivered after a quick forward step (10 cm), with instep/forefoot, usually to ribs/side or head; knees “whip,” strike must “pass through” the target or sound/effect is weak. If the opponent is close—a knee-to-groin variant.
- **Spin kick / spinning back kick:** used when the opponent reacts and closes from the side; stronger than a side kick due to torso rotation, but requires flawless timing—“not recommended for the first time in a fight: lose balance and the opponent has enough time.”
- **Front kick to groin/knee:** the most accurate forward kick; foot travels between the opponent's legs.
- Training targets (reference for Lee training shots): paper sheet, heavy bag, partner-held pad/shield, wooden dummy (muk jong), stick with padded tip, shield thrown into the air.

## 9. Ready choreography sequences (reconstructed from readable text)
1. **Stop-hit against a step into attack:** opponent steps with a windup → Lee, without moving his torso, side-kicks with the foot edge to the front knee/shin → opponent's leg “sticks,” torso pitches forward → finishing backfist/straight to the head. Ending: opponent sinks onto the buckled leg.
2. **Forward burst with feint:** distance 2–2.5 m → flashing upward lead-hand feint near opponent's face (“pulling a rope”) → in the same split second, forward burst and side kick to body → opponent flies backward, Lee lands on one leg and instantly recovers stance.
3. **Counter with sidestep:** opponent throws straight/swing → left sidestep (50–80 cm) without crossing feet, torso completes its turn toward opponent → attack misses → short-line lead straight to jaw. Ending: opponent's momentum carries him past and he is struck as he passes.
4. **Lean back + side kick (against a weapon windup):** club/stick winds up from above-side → Lee shifts weight to rear leg, torso leans outside the radius → in one motion restores weight and side-kicks abdomen → opponent bent over, weapon falling.
5. **Close combat through chi sao:** hands engaged on centerline → Lee “presses through” an opening with flowing pressure → descending backfist from a few centimeters → instant straight with the other hand. Ending: opponent retreats, covering face.
6. **Spin kick against a closing opponent:** opponent reacts to a feint and rushes close from the side → pivot on supporting foot, spinning kick to body → opponent swept back 2+ meters. (Lee: risky technique—can be the climax's “trump card.”)
7. **Retreating trap:** series of quick retreats (opponent accelerates, losing stance) → abrupt stop with backward-forward weight shift → stop-straight or front kick to groin during his step. Ending: opponent runs into the strike at his own speed.

## 10. “Camera” rules from the text (for prompts)
- Lee's silhouette always narrow, side-on; opponents frontal and “open.”
- Before Lee's attack—zero preparatory movement; opponents must wind up/unload weight (telegraph)—the visual code for “who is the master.”
- Every strike “through” the target: contact followed by continued trajectory, opponent displaced bodily rather than just jerking the head.
- After every action Lee returns to stance in 2–3 frames; show instability only in opponents.
