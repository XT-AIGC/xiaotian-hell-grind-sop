# 08 — Tao of Gung Fu (early period)

**Type:** primary-source book (scan, Russian edition FAIR-PRESS 2003, edited by John Little, ~255 pp.)
**Period:** Bruce Lee before JKD—Seattle, early 1960s; foundation—Ip Man's Wing Chun + classical southern styles.
**What it adds to choreography:** The precise “grammar” of early Bruce Lee: narrow Wing Chun bai jong stance with vertical fist at centerline, immovable elbow, close chi-sao combat with simultaneous block-strikes, low stopping kicks. Supplies specific technique vocabulary (pak sao, lop sao, bong sao, tan sao) for hand microchoreography in clinch. Establishes the visual principle of a “continuous river”: movement without pauses, defense flowing into attack. Ideal for “young classical-school fighter” scenes, wooden-dummy training, and “early style vs mature JKD” contrast.

---

## Stances and structure (bai jong)

- Basic ready position—**bai jong**: half frontal, NARROW (narrower than boxing), weight **70% rear leg, 30% front** (up to 80% rear when kicking). Front leg free to defend low kicks and deliver an instant stop-hit.
- Body: chest slightly drawn in, back slightly rounded (without slouching)—“internal school”; abdominal breathing; shoulders and elbows relaxed and lowered.
- Front hand extends toward opponent's nose, elbow bent ~45°, fingers upward; rear hand opposite front elbow provides cover. One hand always covers centerline.
- **Waist is the axis of all movement**: limbs—“small axis,” waist—“large axis”; one hip turn = hundreds of arm rotations. Strike transfers body weight to target; hands merely transmit body's force (arm strength four times less than torso strength).
- Movement: small, quick sliding steps; front foot advances 20–30 cm, rear quickly follows; sideways—cross-step with immediate bai-jong recovery; knee always covers groin.
- Opponent's “seven stars” (weapons to monitor): hand, leg, elbow, head, shoulder, knee, hip. “Three fronts” of attack: before eyes, before hands, before feet.

## Centerline and sticky hands (Wing Chun inheritance)

- **Centerline theory (jong sin lei)**: imaginary vertical from center of head to between legs. Basis of attack and defense; strikes follow it, whole body mass committed through it. Lee's formula: “Establish the center—remember the center—forget the center” (= learn rules, follow them, break them).
- **Immovable-elbow law (bat doan jang)**: front elbow stays 7–8 cm from torso and does NOT shift—like a tornado's stationary center; forearm and hand rotate flexibly around it. Defensive rectangle: groin—eyebrows—shoulders.
- **Four gates**: rectangle divided into 4 zones (upper/lower × outside/inside). Outside gates blocked inside-out, inside gates outside-in; from bai jong—eight basic defensive positions.
- **Chi sao (“sticky hands”)**: Ip Man's method. Partners in forearm contact, elbows NOT spread, hands soft but not yielding—“strong but not rigid.” Rotations like turning a large bus steering wheel. Fighter seeks to remain at center so opponent rotates around him. Energy flows nonstop like a stream; attention stopping = “energy break” and being hit. Mind nowhere means everywhere (wu wei, “be a wooden puppet without a self”).
- Chi-sao combinations: bind opponent's arms by crossing them; center strike; outside strike; under-arm strike; from chi sao—straight punch, backfist, hand-edge chop, “peacock eye.”
- Classic traps: **pak sao** (palm slap-block), **lop sao** (grab-pull of arm with simultaneous stop-hit), **jut sao** (sharp jerking pull/distraction), blocks **tan sao** (palm up), **bong sao** (high elbow block), **fook sao** (covering block), **gan sao** (low outside wrist block).

## Techniques

- **Wing Chun straight punch (chun chui)**—first and foremost: vertical fist, starts FROM CENTER OF CHEST (not hip or shoulder!), flies straight and finishes before opponent's nose on centerline. Lower three knuckles strike; wrist turns through at contact. Fist relaxed to target, “penetrating” strike—target several centimeters DEEPER than visible, strike passes through opponent.
- **Chain punches:** free-flowing strikes one after another on centerline—opponent forced only to defend; combined with finger jab, backfist, shin kick.
- Other hand techniques: finger jab to eyes, “peacock eye” (one knuckle), chopping choy (back of fist), hand-edge strike, chop choy (knuckles), pin chui (horizontal fist), ou choy (arcing strike).
- While one hand strikes, the other ALWAYS remains below striking elbow, covering center/chest.
- **Legs—the most effective weapons** (longer and stronger than arms), but kicks in this period are LOW: no higher than opponent's waist. Targets: groin, knee, shin, bent opponent's chin. Wing Chun classics: heel kick, backward heel kick, low side kick with foot edge, low stamping side kick.
- **Anticipatory (stopping) kick** to advancing opponent's front shin/knee with torso leaning backward—prototype JKD stop-kick.
- High and jumping kicks ONLY for stretching and balance training, “not used in real fights” (important: early Lee himself poses with a high side kick in photos).
- Strike targets: solar plexus, bridge/base of nose, torso, eyes, jaw, groin, abdomen, knee.
- Typical sequences (for sparring staging): opponent strikes right—grab his arm + simultaneous left to temple; palm block + same palm to chest; lop sao pull + stop-straight; leg sweep between opponent's legs + head finish; arm deflected up-out + straight groin kick; block strike + squat with rib strike.
- **Forms**: Sil Lum Dao (“little imagination,” Siu Lim Tao)—108 elements, 3 sections; elbows near center, first third slow and smooth, isometric. Horse stance—foundation of other classical styles. Crane form—wide, beautiful positions (contrast with utilitarian Wing Chun).
- Training equipment for gym scenes: hanging heavy bag, square wall bag filled with peas (nose height), pad on pine board, **muk jong (wooden dummy, classical 108 form)**, metal forearm rings, punching ball, model head, dumbbells, skipping rope. Ceiling-suspended paper sheet—for straight-punch accuracy.

## Movement principles (yin-yang, soft-hard)

- Yin (soft, yielding, defense) and yang (hard, attack) are one whole rather than opposites: “when one hand strikes, the other blocks”—simultaneous block and strike is the style's signature visual feature.
- “Pure yang/pure yin” error: striking while withdrawing other hand = disconnected movement. Block should contain hidden hardness, strike hidden softness.
- **Adaptation law**: do not meet force with force—use opponent's movement and energy against him; a moving person resembles bamboo in wind.
- **Movement like a river**: techniques flow into each other without pause; “to defend is to attack”; movements a string of pearls: techniques are pearls, continuity the string.
- Softness = flexible reed, spring, shadow, flowing water: “Now water can flow—or it can crash. Be water, my friend” (already here!).
- Sparring movement quality: calm mind, head raised, chest drawn in, shoulders/elbows lowered, half squat, every body part harmonious; “movement originates in the waist, not the limbs.”
- “If you feel emptiness—strike straight.” “Hardness cannot reign for long; softness cannot defend”—alternating dominant qualities.
- Fighter against crowd: attention like a football spectator's—the entire field at once, mind never “sticks” to one opponent, switching without thought (image of thousand-armed goddess Guan Yin).

## Differences from mature JKD (for “early style” scenes)

- **Stance**: classical narrow Wing Chun bai jong, 70/30 weight on REAR leg, half-frontal torso—versus JKD's wider, mobile, boxing-like on-guard with springy movement.
- **Range**: early Lee—CLOSE-combat master (chi sao, traps, chest-level clinch); mature JKD adds long range, footwork, interception on approach.
- **Hands**: strictly vertical fist from chest center; JKD later adds boxing hooks, uppercuts, feints, shoulder-origin punch.
- **Legs**: only low utilitarian kicks; JKD—whipping middle-level side kicks, hook kick, front leg used like jab.
- **Form/ritual**: classical forms still present (Sil Lum Dao, dummy 108), horse stance, school-timetable training programs; mature JKD conspicuously rejects forms (“technique number one, pose two—you cannot define an attack that way”).
- Seeds of JKD already visible: “learn the rules—break the rules,” simplification, “bare hand without a beautiful glove,” contempt for “elaborate” styles with extra steps and circling.
- Visual “classical school vs JKD” scene: early fighter stationary/small steps, hands continually in contact with opponent's arms, short vertical punches at point-blank range; JKD fighter separates, bounces, explodes with one long strike.
- Early-period setting: training in Seattle yard/street, black kung-fu uniform or tank top + dark trousers and slipper-like sneakers, wooden dummy, chi sao with Ip Man (book photos), striped trousers in staged straight-punch photos.

## Glossary (book Appendix 2)

jun sin choy—centerline; jut sao—sharp arm jerk; lop sao—hand grab; ou tek—arcing kick; ou choy—arcing hand strike; pak sao—palm strike/block; pin chui—horizontal fist; fook da—hand stays on opponent after striking; ha da—low strike; hou tek—back kick; chop choy—knuckle strike; chun chui—vertical straight fist.

Forms studied/demonstrated by Bruce Lee (Appendix 1): “mantis,” southern “mantis” (main Seattle demonstration form), Wing Chun Sil Lum Dao, Jeet Kuen, Fuzhou “tiger claw,” “crane.” Interests in early 1960s: tai chi, northern “mantis,” bagua, “monkey boxing,” Choy Li Fut.
