# 04 — Bruce Lee's Fighting Method, vol. 2: Basic Training
**Type:** primary-source book (scan, photo sequences; Russian edition 1994, original Bruce Lee's Fighting Method, Ohara 1976)
**What it adds to choreography:** precise phase-by-phase JKD movement mechanics (sliding step, sidestep, “breaking through” distance with progressively smaller steps); the full set of basic hand/leg strikes with trajectories and typical “classical” execution errors for contrast; a three-range system and entry/exit timing; the principle “block = counterstrike in one movement”; a map of vulnerable targets. Photo sequences of 2–4 frames provide ready storyboard reference: readiness → loading → contact → recovery.

## Volume contents (chapters VI–XI in continuous numbering)
VI. The Art of Movement (distance, footwork) — VII. The Art of Punching — VIII. The Art of Kicking — IX. Blocking — X. Targets — XI. Sparring. Text appears in one continuous block (pp. 5–45), followed by ~70 pages of photo plates referenced by number.

## Stance (from photographic material)
- Lee's on-guard: strong (right) hand and leg FORWARD; feet slightly wider than shoulders, front knee bent, rear heel raised—a “spring-loaded” rear leg.
- Torso half-turned toward opponent, narrow silhouette; chin lowered toward front shoulder.
- Lead arm half bent at chest/shoulder level, directed toward opponent's centerline; rear hand at chest/chin provides cover. Hands “alive,” continually moving slightly.
- Both hands cover the body's centerline—the key visual difference from classical stances with fist chambered at hip and torso open.
- A photo shows a low compact stance (half squat, gathered torso)—a close-range/defensive variant.
- Stance errors in photo comparisons: hands too low (upper level open), too high (lower level and torso open), too wide apart (face open), excessively square chest toward opponent.

## Footwork (ch. VI, frame-by-frame mechanics)
- Foundation: a fighter NEVER stands still—constant small maneuvering prevents the opponent judging distance; a moving fighter reacts faster than a stationary one.
- **Sliding advance:** first step short and economical with front foot, rear follows; weight does not bounce, head stays level. A forward step accelerates the strike and conceals intent.
- **Breaking through distance (“seeping,” creeping):** a series of small forward steps, each SHORTER than the last—the opponent loses his sense of distance and suddenly finds himself in striking range.
- **Retreat:** quick gathering step backward just far enough for the strike to miss by centimeters; immediately ready to counter. Best counterattack moment—when the opponent plants his foot after missing (photo: Lee retreats from a kick and immediately answers with the right hand/side kick).
- **Sidestep:** tactic against a linear attack; made at the LAST moment, once the attack has begun and the opponent cannot redirect. Against a right-hander with right side leading—step left; a rightward step is more highly valued in JKD (opponent turns toward you and has no time for a second action) but requires skill. Do not lean or lose rhythm—counter immediately from the step (high side kick, cross).
- **Circling:** small steps around opponent, lead leg as pivot; used to evade a circular kick and enter for a left-hand counter.
- **Running/“fleeting” step (drill):** feet parallel, weight shift, backward step + right side kick; alternate sides without pauses until automatic.
- Timing technique: short rear-leg kick + quick retreat; opponent pursues—repeat: circular movement, forward-back step, then sudden burst with both hands striking.
- Visual markers of poor footwork: wide “jerky” steps, crossed feet, bouncing, freezing in stance, torso “telegraphing” movement.

## Ranges and timing
- Three zones: long (out of reach—safe maneuvering), middle (both can reach with a stepping strike; principal fighting range), close (elbows, knees, trapping; whoever starts first wins).
- Correct fighting distance: at the edge of the opponent's reach, entering with one small step.
- Distance depends on both fighters' speed; an experienced fighter “treads down” the opponent with constant pressure at the zone's boundary.
- Attack when the opponent prepares/changes movement, plants a foot, draws back a hand, inhales, or is psychologically “uncollected.” A strike too early or too late lacks power.
- For a powerful strike, center of gravity transfers forward at contact; after a miss—immediately return to stance.

## Hand strikes (ch. VII)
- **Lead (right) straight—JKD's principal weapon:** starts from the body's CENTER (not hip/shoulder), travels straight along centerline, vertical fist, minimal effort, balance undisturbed. Shortest route to target → fastest.
- Contact with lower three knuckles, wrist straight and firm (bent wrist = injury); forearm like “a mace's handle,” fist its striking head. Fist clenches only at contact; arm relaxed until then.
- Hand returns along the same trajectory to guard—no “hanging” hands (photos: leaving an arm extended invites a counter).
- Rear hand ALWAYS covers during a strike: protects face/torso, ready to block and continue the combination.
- Comparison with classical technique (series A–C, 1–4): classical hip-chambered punch exposes the entire front of the body and travels a long route; JKD punch is faster and covered throughout.
- Combinations: rear straight → left → right-hand finish (photos 45–49); “block—immediately hit with same/other hand.” Maintaining the “central” direction gives an advantage: opponent must go around.
- Hook (“sharp curved fist strike”): no elbow withdrawal or windup, body weight in the punch, a continuation of straights; uppercut against a bent-over opponent.
- Do not “telegraph”: no windup, fist withdrawal, shoulder tension; strike from neutral hand position.
- Do not punch hard surfaces (skull)—open palm/fingers better against the face from above; knuckle conditioning on makiwara.

## Leg strikes (ch. VIII)
- Philosophy: legs strike low (knee, shin, groin)—target closer, difficult to defend, and you remain farther from a counter. “The hand is faster; the leg is stronger and longer.”
- **Basic side kick:** from on-guard, rear foot slides ~10 cm toward front, front knee rises, torso leans away as counterbalance, foot edge/heel strikes toward target; eyes on target, head upright. Retract leg IMMEDIATELY after contact (otherwise it can be caught). Practice on a ~40 kg heavy bag.
- Lead-leg side kick—the fastest kick, a “stopper,” can break through a block and topple; not a feint, used in combinations and defensively against an opponent's entry.
- High/middle side kick: same skip, strike the instant rear foot lands; photo series A–D: hand feint to face → opponent raises hands → side kick to torso.
- **Hook kick (“sharp curved kick”):** faster and more deceptive than side kick, without risking balance; shift rear foot back ~10 cm, rotate hips, whip lower leg in an arc; recover bent leg forward, not backward (photos 41–44). Range closer than side kick but farther than hands.
- **Spin/back kick:** counter against an aggressor driving straight in; 180° turn on ball of supporting foot, eyes retain target to the last moment, torso snaps through at contact, then leg snaps back and balance recovers (most demanding for balance—“several hours of practice before you can even hit the target”).
- **Reverse hook in an arc:** requires flexible legs; sliding skip ~15 cm, leg travels in an almost vertical arc on a small outside-in circle; “slides across the face,” dangerous in shoes.
- **Inside (vertical) groin kick:** short, upward with instep; against an opposite-stance opponent when side kicks cannot reach the groin.
- Large-arc roundhouse is almost never used in JKD—the path is short, power limited.
- Training targets in frames: partner's palm at waist level, heavy bag, pad on stick, paper target. After hitting bag, listen: dry slap = correct, pushing sound = incorrect.
- Do not kick high without warming up; torso back—head upright; in sparring, kick as partner steps backward.

## Blocking (ch. IX)
- Blocking is supplementary: “the best defense is attacking at the moment of attack.” Block at the last moment when the strike can no longer be canceled; early block = opponent changes attack.
- Four basic palm/forearm deflections: high inside (counterclockwise), low inside (downward semicircle + weight onto rear leg), high outside (hand travels “to the other side” across torso), low outside. The block merely DIVERTS the strike—hand stays within body width, otherwise you expose yourself.
- JKD's central principle: block and counterstrike are ONE movement (left deflects, right simultaneously hits face—photos 107–111; against a circular punch—forearm intercepts + right straight). Classical block and counter take two beats; JKD is exactly one beat faster.
- During a series, blocking hand works in sync with rear foot stepping back.
- Against a high kick—retreat with torso lean + catch leg / low counterkick to supporting knee.
- Against low strikes—circular low blocks, without turning the body behind the block.

## Targets (ch. X, 2 “target” diagrams: front and profile)
- Primary (requiring no strength, unprotected by muscle): EYES, GROIN, KNEE/SHIN.
- Knee and shin—the best kicking targets: maximum reach, attack stopper; strike an extended leg at side of knee, behind knee, or thigh.
- Solar plexus—sensitive but usually covered; chin—a good fighter hides it toward shoulder and “meets” the strike with movement; throat—open, struck with extended fingers.
- Open mouth in combat = broken jaw from a light strike (photo example)—amateur marker.
- Striking surfaces: foot edge/heel—side kicks and knee; instep—groin; toe—soft targets only; fist knuckles—soft/middle targets; palm/fingers—face/eyes/throat.

## Sparring and deceptive movements (ch. XI)
- “A fighter without sparring is like a swimmer who has never entered water”: only free sparring establishes distance, timing, and the habit of keeping eyes open.
- Feints: forward step with “live” hands; “long-short,” “short-long-short” rhythms. A feint must look like a real attack (depth + conviction); real strike lands before opponent recovers. Simplest sequences: face feint → abdomen strike; side-kick feint to shin → hook kick to head; fist feint to face → side kick to abdomen; two punches to face → punch to abdomen.
- “Drawing”: deliberately expose a body part, provoke an attack there, and meet it with a counter; retreating bait also works.
- Against a “frozen” opponent who does not respond to feints—simply attack with straight strikes.
- Psychology: patience, do not rush forward after a successful hit; a confident opponent often is unprepared for the first stop-hit; against a wild aggressor—retreat with blocks and catch him on a counter.

## Correct vs incorrect (visual frame markers)
- CORRECT: narrow silhouette; hands on centerline; vertical fist; strike from stance without windup; upright torso, bent knees; limb immediately returns after striking; eyes always on opponent (including during a turn).
- INCORRECT (deliberately illustrated in the book): fist chambered at hip (open torso, “classical kung fu”); windup/withdrawal before strike (“telegraph”); leaning backward while punching (no weight in strike); excessive hip rotation (lost balance); arm left extended; eyes closed/head turned away in defense; open mouth; high straight-legged stance; block “sweeping beyond body width.”
- Style contrast staged in paired frames: left, fighter in classical pose (wide stance, fist at waist); right, Lee in on-guard—a useful pair for an “old school versus JKD” scene.

## Other choreography value
- Photo-sequence structure: almost every technique shot in 2–4 phases against neutral background (beach/gym wall)—direct timeline template: stance → tiny preparatory step → contact → return to stance; opponent has time only to begin reacting between phases.
- Lee repeatedly described from outside: “executes every movement with ease and confidence, strikes effortlessly and withdraws easily; seems to read the opponent”—hero animation benchmark: economy, no preparation, a single explosive beat.
- Poor fighter: “moves unevenly and jerkily, telephones intentions, lets himself be controlled”—opponent animation benchmark.
- Training context for dojo scenes: heavy bag, makiwara, pad on pole, wooden dummy (Wing Chun), paper target, partner holding cushion; sequences of “hit bag—bag swings—hit again in rhythm.”
- The book's distance-based fight structure: long-range maneuvering → feint → explosive single-step entry → 1–3 strikes → instant separation or close-range trapping.
